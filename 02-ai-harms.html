Day 2 · AI harms & risk

Week 1 · Foundations · ~45–60 min

Learning goals

Separate hazards from harms, reason about likelihood and severity, and recognize individual, group, organizational and societal harms.

Risk is contextual

A capability is not a risk assessment. Ask: what can go wrong, who is affected, how likely is it, how severe is it, and what controls exist? A useful structure is hazard → event → affected party → harm → likelihood/severity → controls → residual risk.

Categories of harm

Individual: lost opportunity, privacy invasion, financial or reputational harm. Group: systematically worse performance or exclusion. Organizational: legal, security, operational or trust failures. Societal: manipulation, scaled discrimination or information-ecosystem effects.

Bias enters throughout the lifecycle

Bias can arise from problem formulation, sampling, labels, measurement, proxies, optimization, deployment context or feedback loops. A technically accurate system can still be unfair if the target or decision process is poorly designed.

Risk changes

Users adapt, distributions shift, adversaries probe systems, vendors update models and organizations expand uses. Risk management is therefore a lifecycle rather than a one-time approval.

T&S connection: “92% accurate” tells you little until you know which errors occur, on whose speech, at what scale, with what appeal path and what happens after the classifier fires.

Apply it

A school deploys AI to identify students “at risk of disengagement.” Identify an individual, group, organizational and societal harm, then one control for each.
